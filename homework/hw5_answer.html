1 Textbook Exercises

1.1 Exercise 10.1

  1. Other things being equal, if the tax on a case of beer increases by $2, the number of lives saved is:

\[ 2 \times 0.69 \times 885 = 1221.3 \]

and the 99% confidence interval for the number of lives saved is:

\[ [0.69 \pm Z_{0.005} \times 0.35] \times 2 \times 885 = [-374.53, 2817.13] \]

  1. If New Jersey lowers its drinking age to 19, the predicted decrease in traffic fatalities is:

\[ 0.08 \times 885 = 70.8 \]

and the 95% confidence interval for the number of traffic fatalities is:

\[ [0.08 \pm Z_{0.025} \times 0.07] \times 885 = [-50.62, 192.22] \]

  1. If real income per capita in New Jersey increases by 3%, the predicted increase in traffic fatalities is:

\[ 3 \times 0.01 \times 1.79 \times 885 = 47.52 \]

and the 95% confidence interval for the number of traffic fatalities is:

\[ [1.79 \pm Z_{0.025} \times 0.64] \times 0.01 \times 3 \times 885 = [14.22, 80.83] \]

  1. Standard errors should be clustered into groups within which the values of the dependent variable are likely to be correlated with one another. In this case, the natural candidate group is to cluster by state.

  2. If we believe that drinking age affects the number of fatalities due to alcohol through a linear function, then age should be entered as a continuous variable. If we allow for nonlinearities in this relationship, we should allow for separate intercepts for each age.

1.2 Exercise 10.5

\[ \begin{aligned} Y_{11} &= \beta_1 X_{11} + \alpha_1 + \lambda_1 + u_{11} \\ &= \beta_0 + \beta_1 X_{11} + u_{11} \\ Y_{i1} &= \beta_1 X_{i1} + \alpha_i + \lambda_1 + u_{i1} \\ &= \beta_0 + \beta_1 X_{1i} + \gamma_i + u_{i1}, \quad i = 2, \dots, n \\ Y_{1t} &= \beta_1 X_{1t} + \alpha_1 + \lambda_t + u_{1t} \\ &= \beta_0 + \beta_1 X_{1t} + \delta_t + u_{1t}, \quad t = 2, \dots, T \end{aligned} \]

We have

\[ \begin{aligned} \beta_0 &= \alpha_1 + \lambda_1 \\ \gamma_i &= \alpha_i + \lambda_1 - \beta_0 \\ &= \alpha_i - \alpha_1, \quad i = 2, \dots, n \\ \delta_t &= \alpha_1 + \lambda_t - \beta_0 \\ &= \lambda_t - \lambda_1, \quad t = 2, \dots, T. \end{aligned} \]

1.3 Exercise 11.1

  1. Prepare:

\[ \left\{\begin{aligned} &H_0: \beta_{\text{schooling}} = 0 \\ &H_1: \beta_{\text{schooling}} \neq 0 \end{aligned}\right. \]

Let the significance level be \(\alpha = 0.05\).

Calculate:

\[ t^* = \frac{0.272 - 0}{0.029} \approx 9.379 \]

Conclude:

Since \(|t^*| > Z_{0.05} = 1.96\), we reject \(H_0\). There is significant evidence that schooling affects the probability of working for the government.

  1. The predicted probability that Friedrich will be employed by the government is:

\[ \Phi(-4.107 + 0.272 \times 16) \approx 0.5968 \]

  1. The predicted probability that Hans will get a government job:

\[ \Phi(-4.107 + 0.272 \times 12) \approx 0.1996 \]

  1. The predicted probability that Günter will be employed by the government:

\[ \Phi(-4.107 + 0.272 \times 24) \approx 0.9923 \]

This prediction is likely inaccurate, as the sample did not include individuals with more than 24 years of schooling.

1.5 Exercise 11.5

  1. The predicted probability that Liam will be employed by the government is:

\[ \Phi(-7.702 + 0.548 \times 10 + 4.352 - 0.344 \times 10) \approx 0.0951 \]

  1. The predicted probability that Anneli will be employed by the government is:

\[ \Phi(-7.702 + 0.548 \times 12) \approx 0.1301 \]

  1. Prepare:

\[ \left\{\begin{aligned} &H_0: \beta_{\textit{male} \times \textit{schooling}} = 0 \\ &H_1: \beta_{\textit{male} \times \textit{schooling}} \neq 0 \end{aligned}\right. \]

Let the significance level be \(\alpha = 0.05\).

Calculate:

\[ t^* = \frac{-0.344 - 0}{0.096} \approx -3.583 \]

Conclude:

Since \(|t^*| > Z_{0.05} = 1.96\), we reject \(H_0\). There is significant evidence that the effect of schooling on government employment depends on gender.

2.2 Non-Linear Models Comparison

Consider the following specification:

\[ \textit{insured}_i = \beta_0 + \beta_1 \textit{selfemp}_i + \sum_{k\in\mathcal{I}}\beta_k x_k + u_i \]

where \(\mathcal{I}\) denotes the set of control variables. We propose the hypothesis:

\[ \left\{\begin{aligned} &H_0: \beta_1 = 0 \\ &H_1: \beta_1 \neq 0 \end{aligned}\right. \]

# complete specification
formula_1b <- insured ~ selfemp + age + deg_nd + deg_ged + deg_hs + deg_ba + deg_ma + deg_phd +
  familysz + race_bl + race_wht + male + married

# estimations
LPM_1b <- lm_robust(
  formula = formula_1b,
  data = insurance,
  se_type = "HC1"
)
Logit_1b <- glm(
  formula = formula_1b,
  data = insurance,
  family = binomial(link = "logit")
)
Probit_1b <- glm(
  formula = formula_1b,
  data = insurance,
  family = binomial(link = "probit")
)

2.5 Hypothesis Testing

We propose the following hypothesis:

\[ \left\{\begin{aligned} &H_0: \beta_{\textit{race\_bl}} = \beta_{\textit{race\_wht}} = 0 \\ &H_1: \text{Not all coefficients are zero} \end{aligned}\right. \]

and perform the F-test:

linearHypothesis(
  Logit_1b,
  c("race_bl = 0", "race_wht = 0"),
  vcov = vcovHC,
  type = "HC1",
  test = "F"
)
