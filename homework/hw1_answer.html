1.1 Exercise 2.6

  1. \[ E(Y)=0\cdot 0.12 + 1\cdot 0.88 = 0.88 \]

  2. \[ \Pr(Y=0)=1-\Pr(Y=1) =1-E(Y) =1-0.88=0.12 \]

  3. We first compute the conditional probabilities respectively:

\[ \Pr(Y=0\mid X=0)=\frac{\Pr(X=0,Y=0)}{\Pr(X=0)} =\frac{0.078}{0.751}=\frac{78}{751} \]

\[ \Pr(Y=1\mid X=0)=\frac{\Pr(X=0,Y=1)}{\Pr(X=0)} =\frac{0.673}{0.751}=\frac{673}{751} \]

\[ \Pr(Y=0\mid X=1)=\frac{\Pr(X=1,Y=0)}{\Pr(X=1)} =\frac{0.042}{0.249}=\frac{14}{83} \]

\[ \Pr(Y=1\mid X=1)=\frac{\Pr(X=1,Y=1)}{\Pr(X=1)} =\frac{0.207}{0.249}=\frac{69}{83} \]

and the conditional expectations are:

\[ E(Y\mid X=1)=0\cdot\frac{14}{83}+1\cdot\frac{69}{83} =\frac{69}{83}\approx 0.8313 \]

\[ E(Y\mid X=0)=0\cdot\frac{78}{751}+1\cdot\frac{673}{751} =\frac{673}{751}\approx 0.8961 \]

  1. Unemployment rate for college graduates:

\[ \Pr(Y=0\mid X=1)=1-E(Y\mid X=1)=1-\frac{69}{83}=\frac{14}{83}\approx 0.1687 \]

and for non-college graduates:

\[ \Pr(Y=0\mid X=0)=1-E(Y\mid X=0)=1-\frac{673}{751}=\frac{78}{751}\approx 0.1039 \]

  1. Probability that a randomly selected unemployed worker is a college graduate:

\[ \Pr(X=1\mid Y=0)=\frac{\Pr(X=1,Y=0)}{\Pr(Y=0)}=\frac{0.042}{0.12}=0.35 \]

Probability that a randomly selected unemployed worker is a non-college graduate:

\[ \Pr(X=0\mid Y=0)=\frac{\Pr(X=0,Y=0)}{\Pr(Y=0)}=\frac{0.078}{0.12}=0.65 \]

  1. If \(X\) is independent of \(Y\), then \(P(X\mid Y)=P(X)\).

Because \(\Pr(X=0\mid Y=0)=\frac{78}{751}\neq \Pr(X=0)=0.751\), educational achievement and employment status are not independent.

1.2 Exercise 2.8

We know that \(E(Y)=4\) and \(\mathrm{Var}(Y)=\frac{1}{9}\), hence the expectation and variance of \(Z\) can be computed respectively:

\[ E(Z)=E[3(Y-4)]=3E(Y-4)=3E(Y)-12=3\cdot 4-12=0 \]

\[ \mathrm{Var}(Z)=\mathrm{Var}[3(Y-4)]=3^2\cdot \mathrm{Var}(Y)=9\cdot\frac{1}{9}=1 \]

1.3 Exercise 2.14

By CLT, we have

\[ \bar{Y}\overset{A}{\sim}N\!\left(\mu_Y,\frac{\sigma_Y^2}{n}\right) \]

By the properties of normal distribution, we have

\[ \frac{\bar{Y}-\mu_Y}{\sqrt{\sigma_Y^2/n}}\overset{A}{\sim}N(0,1) \]

  1. \[ \Pr(\bar{Y}\le 51) =\Pr\left(\frac{\bar{Y}-50}{\sqrt{21/50}}\le \frac{51-50}{\sqrt{21/50}}\right) \approx 0.9386 \]

  2. \[ \Pr(\bar{Y}>49) =\Pr\left(\frac{\bar{Y}-50}{\sqrt{21/150}}>\frac{49-50}{\sqrt{21/150}}\right) \approx 0.9962 \]

  3. \[ \Pr(50.5\le \bar{Y}\le 51) =\Pr\left(\frac{50.5-50}{\sqrt{21/45}}\le \frac{\bar{Y}-50}{\sqrt{21/45}}\le \frac{51-50}{\sqrt{21/45}}\right) \approx 0.1605 \]

1.5 Exercise 3.2

  1. \[ \hat p=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n Y_i=\bar Y \]

  2. \[ E(\hat p)=E(\bar Y)=E\!\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n Y_i\right) =\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n E(Y_i) =\frac{1}{n}\cdot np=p \]

  3. \[ \mathrm{Var}(\hat p)=\mathrm{Var}(\bar Y) =\mathrm{Var}\!\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n Y_i\right) =\frac{1}{n^2}\mathrm{Var}\!\left(\sum_{i=1}^n Y_i\right) \]

\[ =\frac{1}{n^2}\bigl[n\cdot \mathrm{Var}(Y_i)\bigr] =\frac{1}{n^2}\cdot np(1-p)=\frac{p(1-p)}{n} \]

1.6 Exercise 3.3

  1. \[ \hat p=\frac{270}{500}=0.54 \]

  2. The estimated variance of \(\hat p\) is: \[ \mathrm{Var}(\hat p)=\frac{\hat p(1-\hat p)}{n} =\frac{0.54(1-0.54)}{500}=0.0004968 \]

and the standard error is: \[ SE(\hat p)=\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}(\hat p)}\approx 0.0223 \]

  1. First derive the pivotal quantity: \[ z=\frac{\hat p-p_0}{\sqrt{p_0(1-p_0)/n}} \;\overset{A}{\sim}\; N(0,1) \]

then, calculate the \(z\)-statistic: \[ z^*=\frac{0.54-0.5}{\sqrt{0.5(1-0.5)/500}}\approx 1.7889 \]

The \(p\)-value is: \[ p\text{-value}=2\cdot \Phi(-z^*)\approx 2\cdot \Phi(-1.7889)\approx 0.0736 \]

  1. For a one-sided test: \[ p\text{-value}=1-\Phi(z^*)\approx 1-\Phi(1.7889)\approx 0.0368 \]

  2. Part (c) is a two-sided test, where the \(p\)-value is the area in the tails of the standard normal distribution outside \(\pm z^*\).
    Part (d) is a one-sided test, where the \(p\)-value is the area under the standard normal distribution to the right of the calculated \(z^*\).

  3. For the test \(H_0:p=0.5\) vs. \(H_1:p>0.5\) at \(\alpha=0.05\), we reject \(H_0\) because the \(p\)-value is less than the significance level. There is statistically significant evidence that the democratic candidate was ahead of the republican candidate at the time of conducting the poll.

  1. \[ \hat p \pm Z_{0.025}\cdot SE(\hat p) =0.54 \pm 1.96\cdot 0.0223 =[0.4963,\,0.5837] \]

  2. \[ \hat p \pm Z_{0.005}\cdot SE(\hat p) =0.54 \pm 2.576\cdot 0.0223 =[0.4826,\,0.5974] \]

  3. Mechanically, the interval in part (b) is wider because of a larger critical value. Substantively, a 99% confidence interval is wider than a 95% confidence interval because a 99% confidence interval must contain the true value of \(p\) in 99% of all possible samples, while a 95% confidence interval must contain the true value of \(p\) in only 95% of all possible samples.

  4. Because \(0.5\in\) C.I., we do not reject \(H_0\) at a 5% significance level.

1.8 Exercise 3.9

  1. The size of a test is the probability of erroneously rejecting a null hypothesis when it is valid. We know: \[ E(Y)=1000,\quad \sigma_Y=100,\quad n=50. \]

Then, \[ E(\bar Y)=1000,\qquad \sigma_{\bar Y}=\frac{\sigma_Y}{\sqrt{n}}=\frac{100}{\sqrt{50}}=10\sqrt{2}. \]

Now, \[ \text{size}=\Pr(\bar Y>1100\mid \mu=1000) =\Pr\left(\frac{\bar Y-1000}{\sigma_{\bar Y}}>\frac{1100-1000}{\sigma_{\bar Y}}\right)\approx 0. \]

  1. The power of a test is the probability of correctly rejecting a null hypothesis when it is invalid. First, we calculate the probability of a Type II error: \[ \beta=\Pr(\bar Y\le 1100\mid \mu=1150) =\Pr\left(\frac{\bar Y-1150}{\sigma_{\bar Y}}\le \frac{1100-1150}{\sigma_{\bar Y}}\right)\approx 0.0002. \]

Then, the power of the test is: \[ 1-\beta \approx 0.9998. \]

  1. For a test with size 1%, the rejection region for \(H_0\) contains values of the \(z\)-statistic exceeding \(Z_{0.01}\). That is, \[ t^*=\frac{\bar Y-1000}{10\sqrt{2}} > Z_{0.01}=2.326. \]

Thus, \[ \bar Y > 1000+10\sqrt{2}\cdot 2.326 \approx 1032.89. \]

So, the manager should believe the inventor’s claim if the sample mean life of the new product is greater than 1032.89 hours to maintain a test size of 1%.

1.9 Exercise 3.13

  1. \[ \bar Y \pm Z_{0.05}\cdot \frac{\sigma_Y}{\sqrt{n}} =712.1 \pm 1.645\cdot \frac{23.2}{\sqrt{400}} =[710.19,\,714.01] \]

  2. Prepare: \[ \begin{cases} H_0:\ \mu_1-\mu_2=0\\ H_A:\ \mu_1-\mu_2\neq 0 \end{cases} \]

where \(\mu_1\) is the average salary in small class districts, and \(\mu_2\) is the average salary in large class districts. Let the significance level be \(\alpha=0.05\).

Calculate: \[ t^*=\frac{(\bar Y_1-\bar Y_2)-0}{\sqrt{\frac{s_1^2}{n_1}+\frac{s_2^2}{n_2}}} =\frac{721.8-710.9}{\sqrt{\frac{21.4^2}{110}+\frac{20.6^2}{250}}} \approx 4.5790 \]

Conclude: Since \(t^*>Z_{0.025}=1.96\), we reject \(H_0\). There is statistically significant evidence that districts with smaller class sizes have higher average test scores.

(Sportscards.xlsx)

# clear the mamory and import package
rm(list = ls())
library(tidyverse)
library(readxl)

card <- read_excel("data/Sportscards.xlsx")

2.1 Theoretical Trade Result

  1. A person will trade if she received good A but prefers good B or she received good B and prefers good A. 50% received good A, of these 0% prefer good B. 50% receive good B, of these 100% prefer good A. Thus, the expected fraction traded is:

\[ 0.5 \times 0 + 0.5 \times 1 = 0.5 \]

  1. A person will trade if she received good A but prefers good B or she received good B and prefers good A. 50% received good A, of these 50% prefer good B; 50% receive good B, of these 50% prefer good A. Thus, the expected fraction traded is:

\[ 0.5 \times 0.5 + 0.5 \times 0.5 = 0.5 \]

  1. A person will trade if she received good A but prefers good B or she received good B and prefers good A. 50% received good A, of these \((100 - X)\%\) prefer good B; 50% receive good B, of these \(X\%\) prefer good A. Thus, the expected fraction traded is:

\[ 0.5 \times (100 - X)\% + 0.5 \times X\% = 0.5 \]

2.2 Empirical Trade Result

Denote the probability of subjects trading their goods as \(p\). We propose the following hypothesis:

\[ \begin{cases} H_0: p = 0.5 & \text{(no endowment effect)} \\ H_A: p \ne 0.5 & \text{(endowment effect)} \end{cases} \]

and perform the t-test:

t.test(
  card$trade,
  mu = 0.5,
  alternative = "two.sided"
)

    One Sample t-test

According to the testing result (p-value \(< 0.05\)), we reject the null hypothesis at 5% significance level. Hence there is statistically significant evidence of an endowment effect.

2.3 Empirical Trade Result: Subgroup

2.3.1 Dealers

Denote the probability of dealer subjects trading their goods as \(p_d\). We propose the following hypothesis and perform the same testing again:

\[ \begin{cases} H_0: p_d = 0.5 & \text{(no endowment effect for dealer)} \\ H_A: p_d \ne 0.5 & \text{(endowment effect for dealer)} \end{cases} \]

card_dealer <- card %>% filter(dealer == 1)
t.test(
  card_dealer$trade, 
  mu = 0.5,
  alternative = "two.sided"
)

    One Sample t-test

data:  card_dealer$trade
t = -0.92912, df = 73, p-value = 0.3559
alternative hypothesis: true mean is not equal to 0.5
95 percent confidence interval:
 0.3299982 0.5618937
sample estimates:
mean of x 
0.4459459

According to the testing result (p-value = 0.3524), we do not reject the null hypothesis at 5% significance level. Hence there is no evidence of an endowment effect for dealer subjects.

2.3.2 Non-dealers

Denote the probability of non-dealer subjects trading their goods as \(p_n\). We propose the following hypothesis and perform the same testing again:

\[ \begin{cases} H_0: p_n = 0.5 & \text{(no endowment effect for non-dealer)} \\ H_A: p_n \ne 0.5 & \text{(endowment effect for non-dealer)} \end{cases} \]

card_nondealer <- card %>% filter(dealer == 0)
t.test(
  card_nondealer$trade, 
  mu = 0.5,
  alternative = "two.sided"
)

    One Sample t-test

95 percent confidence interval:

sample estimates:
mean of x 
0.2297297

According to the testing result (p-value \(< 0.05\)), we reject the null hypothesis at 5% significance level. Hence there is statistically significant evidence of an endowment effect on non-dealer subjects.

2.3.3 Group difference

To investigate into the group difference in behavior, we propose the following hypothesis:

\[ \begin{cases} H_0: p_d - p_n = 0 & \text{(no group difference in behavior)} \\ H_A: p_d - p_n \ne 0 & \text{(group difference in behavior)} \end{cases} \]

Since we can’t determine the distribution of \(p_d - p_n\), we utilize the CLT and perform the t-test:

t.test(
  card$trade ~ card$dealer,
  conf.level = 0.95,
  alternative = "two.sided"
)

    Welch Two Sample t-test

sample estimates:
mean in group 0 mean in group 1 
      0.2297297       0.4459459

According to the testing result (p-value \(< 0.05\)), we reject the null hypothesis at 5% significance level. Hence there is statistically significant difference in the behavior between dealer and non-dealer.
